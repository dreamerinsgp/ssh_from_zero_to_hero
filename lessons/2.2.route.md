Q1: What is Route?

**Answer:** Think of a route like directions on a GPS navigation system. When you want to drive from your home (source) to a friend's house (destination), your GPS calculates the best path through various streets and highways (routers/networks).

In networking:
- **Route** = The path your data packets take from your computer to a destination server
- **Routers** = Like intersections or junctions that decide which direction to send your data
- **Routing table** = Like a GPS's map database that knows all the possible paths

Just like:
- A GPS chooses the fastest or shortest route based on traffic conditions
- Routers choose the best network path based on network conditions, distance, and availability

When you connect from China to Singapore:
- **Direct route** might be like taking a long, congested highway (high latency, packet loss)
- **Route through Hong Kong** (ProxyJump) is like taking a better path with less traffic (lower latency, more reliable)

The route determines how your data travels across the internet, and choosing a good route (like using ProxyJump) can make your connection much faster and more reliable.


Q2: What Routers are?

**Answer:** Routers are like intelligent traffic directors at intersections in a city. Just as a traffic officer directs cars to different roads based on their destination, routers direct data packets to different network paths based on where they need to go.

In networking:
- **Router** = A network device that forwards data packets between computer networks
- **Routing** = The process of selecting the best path for data to travel
- **Routing decision** = Like choosing which exit to take at a highway junction based on your destination

Think of it this way:
- When you send an email or browse a website, your data is broken into small packets
- Each packet needs to find its way from your computer to the destination server
- Routers along the way examine each packet's destination address (like reading a package label)
- They check their routing tables (like a GPS map) to determine the next best hop
- They forward the packet to the next router closer to the destination

Just like:
- A postal worker at a sorting facility looks at an address and routes mail to the correct regional center
- Routers look at IP addresses and route packets toward the correct network
- Multiple routers work together, each forwarding packets one step closer until they reach their destination

When connecting from China to Singapore:
- Your packet might go: Your Computer → Router A (China) → Router B (Hong Kong) → Router C (Singapore) → Destination Server
- Each router makes a decision: "Based on my routing table, the best next step is Router B"
- This happens automatically and very quickly (in milliseconds)

Routers are essential because they:
1. **Connect different networks** - They link your home network to your ISP's network, which connects to the global internet
2. **Find optimal paths** - They choose routes based on speed, reliability, and network conditions
3. **Handle traffic efficiently** - They can balance load across multiple paths to avoid congestion
4. **Enable internet communication** - Without routers, data wouldn't know how to navigate the complex web of interconnected networks 


Q3: What determines the number of hops in a connection ? 

**Answer:** The number of hops in a connection is determined by the network topology and routing decisions made along the path from source to destination. Think of it like the number of stops on a train journey - it depends on how many stations (routers) your data needs to pass through to reach its destination.

**Example:**
```
tracert 118.194.235.63

Tracing route to 118.194.235.63 over a maximum of 30 hops

  1     7 ms     2 ms     1 ms  192.168.71.1
  2     4 ms     4 ms     4 ms  100.68.100.1
  3     3 ms     6 ms     5 ms  61.172.75.177
  4     *        *        *     Request timed out.
  5     *        *        *     Request timed out.
```

**Factors that determine the number of hops:**

1. **Geographic Distance**
   - Local connections (same city): Usually 3-5 hops
   - National connections (same country): Usually 5-10 hops
   - International connections: Usually 10-20+ hops
   - Like taking more connecting flights when traveling farther

2. **Network Architecture**
   - **Tier 1 ISPs** (major backbone providers): Fewer hops, more direct routes
   - **Tier 2/3 ISPs** (regional/local providers): More hops, may route through multiple networks
   - **Enterprise networks**: May have internal routing that adds hops

3. **Routing Policies**
   - **Peering agreements**: ISPs may route through specific partners
   - **Traffic engineering**: Networks may intentionally route through certain paths
   - **Cost optimization**: Cheaper routes may have more hops

4. **Network Topology**
   - **Direct peering**: Fewer hops when networks connect directly
   - **Transit relationships**: More hops when routing through intermediate providers
   - **Redundancy**: Backup paths may have different hop counts

5. **Physical Infrastructure**
   - **Submarine cables**: International connections may route through cable landing points
   - **Satellite links**: May add hops through ground stations
   - **Regional hubs**: Data centers and internet exchange points (IXPs) affect routing

**In the example above:**
- Hop 1 (192.168.71.1): Your local router/gateway
- Hop 2 (100.68.100.1): Your ISP's router
- Hop 3 (61.172.75.177): Next network hop (possibly regional ISP)
- Hops 4-5: Timeouts (routers configured not to respond to traceroute, but still forwarding packets)

**Why hops matter:**
- **More hops** = More potential points of failure, higher latency
- **Fewer hops** = More direct path, typically lower latency (but not always - a direct path with congestion can be slower than a longer path with better bandwidth)
- **Each hop adds latency**: Typically 1-50ms per hop depending on distance and network conditions

The number of hops is not always directly correlated with speed - a 5-hop route through fast networks can be faster than a 3-hop route through congested networks.



Q4: What is ISP?

**Answer:** ISP stands for **Internet Service Provider** - think of it as the company that gives you access to the internet, like how a utility company provides you with electricity or water to your home.

In networking:
- **ISP** = A company or organization that provides internet access to customers
- **Your ISP** = The company you pay monthly to connect your home/office to the internet
- **Tier 1 ISP** = Major backbone providers that form the core of the internet infrastructure
- **Tier 2/3 ISP** = Regional or local providers that connect to Tier 1 ISPs

Think of it this way:
- **Your home network** → Connected to → **Your ISP** → Connected to → **Other ISPs** → Connected to → **Global Internet**
- Just like your local post office connects to regional postal centers, which connect to national postal networks
- ISPs are the "on-ramps" to the internet highway system

**Types of ISPs:**

1. **Tier 1 ISPs** (Backbone Providers)
   - Own and operate major internet infrastructure (undersea cables, fiber networks)
   - Examples: AT&T, Verizon, Level 3, Cogent
   - They peer with each other (exchange traffic directly) without paying
   - Form the core backbone of the global internet

2. **Tier 2 ISPs** (Regional Providers)
   - Connect to Tier 1 ISPs and provide service to smaller regions
   - May peer with other Tier 2 ISPs
   - Often pay Tier 1 ISPs for transit (access to the rest of the internet)

3. **Tier 3 ISPs** (Local Providers)
   - Small, local internet service providers
   - Connect to Tier 2 or Tier 1 ISPs
   - Provide service directly to end users (homes, businesses)

**In the context of routing (from earlier examples):**
- When you trace a route: `tracert 118.194.235.63`
- Hop 1 (192.168.71.1): Your local router
- Hop 2 (100.68.100.1): **Your ISP's router** - This is where your ISP takes over
- Hop 3 (61.172.75.177): Another ISP's router (possibly regional or Tier 1)
- Each hop represents a router owned by different ISPs or network operators

**Why ISPs matter for your connection:**
- **Quality of service**: Better ISPs have better infrastructure, lower latency, higher reliability
- **Routing decisions**: Your ISP determines the initial path your data takes
- **Peering agreements**: ISPs with better peering can provide faster routes to popular destinations
- **Geographic location**: Your ISP's location affects routing - connecting through Hong Kong ISP vs. mainland China ISP can result in very different routes to Singapore

**Real-world analogy:**
- **ISP** = Like your local phone company or cable company
- **Tier 1 ISP** = Like major long-distance carriers that own the infrastructure
- **Your connection** = You pay your ISP monthly, they provide you internet access, and they route your traffic through their network to reach destinations on the internet

When you use ProxyJump through Hong Kong, you're essentially routing your traffic through a different ISP (Hong Kong-based) which may have better peering agreements and routing paths to your destination (Singapore) compared to routing directly through your original ISP.
